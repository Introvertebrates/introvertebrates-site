<h2>Four spiders confined to mountain islands</h2>

<p>
  In Western Australia's Koi Kyenunu-ruff, also known as the Stirling Range, four species of <em>Cataxia</em> trapdoor spider live in cool, moist pockets high among the peaks. Each is a short-range endemic: <em>Cataxia sandsorum</em>, <em>C. stirlingi</em>, <em>C. colesi</em> and <em>C. barrettae</em> occur naturally in extremely limited areas and nowhere else.
</p>

<p>
  These are not spiders built for rapid escape or recolonisation. A young <em>Cataxia</em> establishes a silk-lined burrow and may remain close to it for years. Females are long-lived, while dispersal between isolated mountains and gullies is limited. The same traits that allowed distinct species to persist in separate “sky islands” also leave them dangerously exposed when those refuges shrink.
</p>

<h2>Returning after 22 years</h2>

<p>
  Researchers returned to sites surveyed in 2002 and repeated searches across more than 250 kilometres of rugged terrain. They counted and mapped the spiders' characteristic burrow entrances, then used those observations to estimate changes in abundance and occupied area.
</p>

<p>
  Across all four species, estimated burrow abundance had fallen by more than <strong>98%</strong>. The area occupied by each species had contracted by approximately <strong>49–67%</strong>. Places that once held dense clusters of burrows now contained scattered survivors—or no visible burrows at all.
</p>

<p>
  Those figures describe estimated change in burrow abundance, not a census in which every spider was individually counted. Burrows are the practical sign researchers can survey for these hidden animals, and statistical models help account for what may be missed. That distinction matters, but it does not make the pattern reassuring: four independently restricted species all showed severe decline over the same 22-year interval.
</p>

<h2>The youngest spiders are disappearing too</h2>

<p>
  A surviving adult can make a trapdoor-spider population look more secure than it is. Mygalomorph spiders can live for many years, and an established female may persist in her burrow even when reproduction or juvenile survival has begun to fail.
</p>

<p>
  In many of the remaining populations, researchers found very few juveniles or newly established burrows. That shortage of recruits is especially worrying. If older females remain while too few young spiders replace them, the visible population may be recording its past rather than securing its future.
</p>

<p>
  This is one reason long-lived invertebrates can decline quietly. Their disappearance does not necessarily arrive as a sudden crash. It can unfold as an ageing collection of survivors, increasingly isolated from one another, with fewer young spiders appearing after each breeding season.
</p>

<h2>Fire, drying and shrinking refuges</h2>

<p>
  The steepest losses were associated with lower elevations and sites exposed to repeated fire. Surviving populations were increasingly concentrated in cool, moist, high-elevation gullies—places where rock, shade and local topography can soften heat and drying and offer some protection from flames.
</p>

<p>
  Fire can change more than the vegetation above a burrow. Intense or repeated burns can remove shade and leaf litter, dry the soil, destabilise steep slopes and alter the humid microclimate these spiders depend on. A population may survive the immediate passage of fire yet lose the sheltered conditions needed for juveniles to establish afterwards.
</p>

<p>
  Climate drying can amplify that pressure. As cooler habitats contract uphill, species already restricted to individual peaks have nowhere higher to move. The remaining gullies become climatic refuges: small remnants of suitable habitat surrounded by increasingly hot, dry and fire-prone country.
</p>

<p>
  The study found strong associations between decline, elevation and fire history, but it cannot assign one simple cause to every missing population. Fire, long-term drying, habitat change, poor recruitment and isolation can interact. Conservation therefore needs to protect the refuges and investigate the mechanisms, rather than treating a single threat as the whole explanation.
</p>

<h2>Ancient lineages with very little room</h2>

<p>
  Trapdoor spiders are often described as ancient because mygalomorph lineages reach deep into spider evolutionary history. That does not make the present species unchanged “living fossils,” but it does underline how much evolutionary history can be concentrated in a tiny geographic range.
</p>

<p>
  The four <em>Cataxia</em> species were shaped by long isolation among the Stirling Range's peaks. Losing one would mean losing a lineage found on no other mountain and in no other country. Their restricted ranges also mean that a single severe fire or prolonged dry period can affect a large fraction of an entire species.
</p>

<h2>What protection would need to do</h2>

<p>
  The remaining high-elevation gullies deserve particular attention. Protecting them may require carefully planned fire management, avoiding repeated intense burns, retaining shade and soil moisture, monitoring recruitment and identifying microhabitats that continue to support juveniles.
</p>

<p>
  Restoring connectivity may help where suitable habitat can be linked, but these spiders are naturally poor dispersers and the mountain populations have been isolated for long periods. Moving spiders or mixing populations would therefore require strong genetic and ecological evidence rather than a hurried rescue attempt.
</p>

<p>
  Continued surveys are essential. Burrow counts, age structure and microclimate monitoring can reveal whether surviving populations are stabilising, still contracting or failing to replace old adults. Formal conservation assessments may also follow, but the new study does not by itself grant all four species a particular legal or IUCN category.
</p>

<h2>Still present is not the same as safe</h2>

<p>
  The most important lesson from the <em>Cataxia</em> study is easy to miss. A long-lived spider can remain in its burrow for years after the conditions that sustained its population have begun to vanish. Finding an adult proves that one spider survived; it does not prove that enough young spiders are coming behind her.
</p>

<p>
  In the Stirling Range, the remaining burrows are concentrated in the coolest, wettest fragments of a shrinking refuge. The watchers are still there—but after losses of this scale, simply watching them disappear is no longer enough.
</p>
