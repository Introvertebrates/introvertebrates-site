<p>
  In 2022, translucent jumping-spider young offered researchers a rare view inside a resting spider's head. Their retinas moved in regular bouts while their legs curled and their bodies twitched—a pattern strikingly similar to features of rapid eye movement sleep.
</p>

<p>
  The discovery was compelling, but one central question remained: <strong>were the spiders genuinely asleep?</strong> Visible movements alone could not establish that the state was regulated sleep rather than an unusual form of quiet rest.
</p>

<p>
  A new study of the jumping spider <em>Portia fimbriata</em> has now supplied the missing experiments. The researchers found circadian timing, organised sleep architecture, reduced responsiveness during REM-like periods and recovery sleep after disruption. Together, these independent tests support a behavioural demonstration of <strong>two-stage sleep</strong> in a spider.
</p>

<h2>What scientists need before calling a state sleep</h2>

<p>
  Remaining still at night is not enough. Behavioural sleep is normally identified through several converging criteria: a regular rest–activity rhythm, a characteristic resting posture, reduced responsiveness that can still be reversed, and compensation after sleep has been disrupted.
</p>

<p>
  The 2026 study tested these criteria in <em>P. fimbriata</em>, a visually guided jumping spider from tropical Queensland. Its nocturnal resting posture and daytime activity made it suitable for separating ordinary inactivity from a regulated sleep state.
</p>

<h2>A clock that continued without external cues</h2>

<p>
  The spiders were active mainly during the day, and their rest–activity cycle continued under constant conditions. That free-running rhythm indicates that the timing was generated internally rather than being only a direct reaction to changing light.
</p>

<p>
  Their nights were also structured. Quiet sleep alternated with recurring REM-like episodes, producing two recognisable behavioural stages rather than one undifferentiated period of immobility.
</p>

<h2>Harder to wake during the REM-like stage</h2>

<p>
  A sleeping animal should respond less readily than an awake one while remaining capable of waking. The researchers tested arousal thresholds and found reduced responsiveness during the REM-like periods.
</p>

<p>
  This matters because it links the visible twitching state to sleep itself. The 2022 observations showed a remarkable recurring pattern; the new response tests demonstrate that the pattern occurs while the spider is in a state of reduced arousal.
</p>

<h2>Lost sleep was followed by rebound</h2>

<p>
  Sleep is also regulated homeostatically: disrupting it creates pressure that the animal later compensates for. After the spiders' sleep was disturbed, they showed rebound effects.
</p>

<p>
  Circadian control, state-dependent responsiveness and rebound are separate lines of evidence. Their agreement is what turns the case from “these spiders look as though they may have REM-like sleep” into a behavioural demonstration that they sleep in two stages.
</p>

<h2>REM-like does not mean human REM</h2>

<p>
  The study measured behaviour, not electrical activity inside the spiders' brains. “REM-like” therefore describes functional and visible parallels—recurring active episodes within sleep and reduced responsiveness—not proof that the underlying neural state is identical to vertebrate REM sleep.
</p>

<p>
  It also does not show that spiders dream. Dreaming is a claim about subjective experience, and neither twitching, retinal movement nor rebound can reveal what a spider experiences while asleep.
</p>

<h2>Why spider sleep matters</h2>

<p>
  Most detailed sleep research has focused on vertebrates and a small group of laboratory invertebrates. Jumping spiders have a very different nervous system and sensory world, yet they show regulated sleep divided into quiet and REM-like stages.
</p>

<p>
  That makes them valuable for asking how often active sleep has evolved, what functions it serves and whether very different nervous systems arrive at similar sleep architecture independently. The answer is no longer simply that sleeping spiders twitch. We now have behavioural evidence that those movements belong to a genuine, regulated sleep state.
</p>
