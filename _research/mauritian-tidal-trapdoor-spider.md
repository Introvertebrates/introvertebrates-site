<h2>A trapdoor at the edge of the ocean</h2>

<p>
  Most trapdoor spiders are associated with burrows in soil, leaf litter or sheltered banks. <em>Idioctis marevallum</em> lives somewhere far less forgiving: the intertidal edge of two small islands off Mauritius, where the same patch of rock can be dry, hit by waves and later covered by the sea.
</p>

<p>
  The spider occupies narrow tubes in coralline rock. These cavities were not necessarily excavated by the spider itself; the researchers suggest that marine animals such as molluscs or worms may originally have bored them into the rock. The spider lines a tube with silk and builds a hinged door that blends into the surrounding shore.
</p>

<p>
  That door includes silk, coarse sand and pieces of algae. In photographs it can be extremely difficult to distinguish from the rock around it—until a pair of legs appears at the entrance.
</p>

<h2>A home that high tide can cover</h2>

<p>
  The researchers documented shelters in the intertidal zone that were repeatedly struck by waves or entirely submerged during high tide. That makes <em>I. marevallum</em> an unusual example of a mygalomorph spider living at the boundary between land and sea.
</p>

<p>
  What happens inside the closed tube while the water is above it remains an open question. The study describes the habitat and burrow structure, but it did not test how much water enters the shelter, whether air is retained, or how the spider tolerates submersion. It would therefore be premature to say that the spider breathes underwater or possesses a proven watertight door.
</p>

<p>
  The remarkable observation is simpler and well supported: the spider has established a working home in a place where a terrestrial animal's shelter can temporarily disappear beneath the sea.
</p>

<h2>Found during island-restoration work</h2>

<p>
  The first spiders were encountered in 2023 during night surveys on Îlot Vacoas, a protected islet of roughly one hectare. Researchers initially wondered whether they had rediscovered <em>Tigidia mauriciana</em>, a Mauritian trapdoor spider known from an old description. Closer examination showed that the animals belonged to a different genus and represented a species new to science.
</p>

<p>
  Later surveys found the same species on Île aux Fouquets, another small offshore island in Mauritius's south-eastern lagoon. The scientific name <em>marevallum</em> is derived from Latin for “sea wall,” a reference to the spider's wave-battered habitat.
</p>

<p>
  The formal description is based on female specimens. As with many newly described invertebrates, basic questions about its life remain unanswered: the male has not yet been described, its diet and reproductive biology are unknown, and its full distribution may be wider than the two confirmed islands.
</p>

<h2>One spider inside a much larger survey</h2>

<p>
  The new trapdoor spider was part of a wider study of Mauritian spiders. The researchers recorded 14 species that had not previously been documented from the archipelago, increasing its known spider diversity by <strong>27.45%</strong>.
</p>

<p>
  Some of those additions may reflect species arriving or being transported between islands, while others reveal native diversity that had simply escaped scientific attention. Either way, the result shows how incomplete an island inventory can remain—even for animals living close to places surveyed repeatedly for other wildlife.
</p>

<p>
  Offshore islets are especially important in Mauritius because they can retain habitats and endemic species lost or heavily altered on the main island. Finding an unusual spider during restoration work demonstrates another benefit of long-term conservation: people looking after an ecosystem are also in a position to notice what science has not yet recorded.
</p>

<h2>Rare does not automatically mean endangered</h2>

<p>
  <em>Idioctis marevallum</em> is currently known from only two very small islands, which makes its restricted confirmed range scientifically and conservationally important. But a newly described species is not automatically endangered, and the paper does not provide a population estimate or formal threat assessment.
</p>

<p>
  Coastal disturbance, invasive species and extreme weather could plausibly matter to such a specialised animal, but those risks still need to be investigated rather than assumed. The immediate priority is to learn where else the spider occurs, how many suitable rock-tube habitats remain, and how its populations respond to environmental change.
</p>

<h2>A discovery hiding in plain tide</h2>

<p>
  The most striking part of this story is not simply that a new spider received a name. It is that a close relative of tarantulas has made a home in a zone most terrestrial spiders avoid—behind a door camouflaged with material from the shore, waiting as the sea advances and retreats around it.
</p>

<p>
  Its physiology is still mysterious, its population is uncounted and even the male is unknown. Those gaps do not weaken the discovery. They show how much natural history can remain concealed behind something as small as a trapdoor in a rock.
</p>
