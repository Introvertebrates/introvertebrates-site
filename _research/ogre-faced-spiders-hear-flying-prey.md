<h2>A hunter with two ways to strike</h2>

<p>
  Ogre-faced spiders do not wait for prey to blunder into a permanent web. A hunting <em>Deinopis spinosa</em> hangs within a sparse silk frame while holding a small, stretchy net between its front four legs.
</p>

<p>
  Ground-dwelling prey beneath the spider can be captured with a forward lunge. This attack relies heavily on the spider's enormous posterior median eyes, which are exceptionally sensitive in low light. Flying insects present a different problem: they may approach from behind or above, outside the area the spider is watching.
</p>

<p>
  To intercept them, the spider performs a fast backward strike—twisting overhead and casting its net at the passing insect. Researchers suspected that airborne sound might provide the missing cue. Their experiments showed that the spider's legs can indeed detect it.
</p>

<h2>Hearing without ears</h2>

<p>
  Spiders do not have eardrums like ours. Instead, sensitive structures on their legs respond to the movement of air particles produced by sound. In the ogre-faced spider, a metatarsal organ near the tip of each leg appears to play an important role.
</p>

<p>
  The researchers recorded electrical activity both from the brains of intact spiders and from isolated legs while playing tones between <strong>100 and 10,000 hertz</strong>. Neural responses appeared across that broad frequency range, showing that airborne sound could be detected by the legs and represented in the nervous system.
</p>

<p>
  The spiders responded at sound levels of 60 decibels or more from a speaker two metres away—the greatest distance tested. That does not establish their maximum range, but it shows that the sense can operate well beyond the spider's immediate reach.
</p>

<h2>Low sounds triggered the net</h2>

<p>
  Detecting a tone is not the same as using it to hunt, so the researchers also tested freely behaving spiders. Low-frequency tones at 150, 400 and 750 hertz sometimes triggered backward net-casting strikes. These frequencies overlap with the wingbeat sounds of moths, mosquitoes and flies recorded as natural prey.
</p>

<p>
  Higher-frequency tones at 2, 3, 5 and 10 kilohertz produced neural responses but did not provoke the same backward attack. The distinction matters: the spiders could detect more frequencies than they treated as a reason to throw the net.
</p>

<p>
  The authors proposed that sensitivity to higher frequencies might help the spiders notice predators such as birds, but that idea was not tested in this study. It remains a hypothesis rather than an observed anti-predator response.
</p>

<h2>What does “hearing” mean for a spider?</h2>

<p>
  Calling this hearing is useful, but it should not imply a tiny human-style ear hidden on each leg. Sound is a pressure wave accompanied by air-particle movement. Fine hairs and other mechanical receptors can respond to that movement, allowing a spider to extract information from airborne vibrations without an eardrum.
</p>

<p>
  The behavioural trials showed that sound alone could trigger the characteristic aerial-prey attack. They did not establish exactly how accurately a spider can locate an insect, how several cues are combined during a real hunt, or whether it recognises particular prey species by sound.
</p>

<h2>More than an impressive pair of eyes</h2>

<p>
  Ogre-faced spiders already seemed built around vision: their two largest eyes dominate the face and support remarkably precise nocturnal attacks on prey below. The sound experiments reveal a more flexible sensory hunter. Vision covers the ground-facing strike zone, while low-frequency airborne cues can alert the spider to prey flying through a very different part of its surroundings.
</p>

<p>
  The spectacular backward flip is therefore more than acrobatics. It is the visible end of a sensory chain that begins when moving air reaches the spider's legs—and ends with a flying insect inside a handheld net.
</p>
