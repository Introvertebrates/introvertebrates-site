<h2>A sting with an unresolved purpose</h2>

<p>In several scorpion groups, males sting females during courtship. The behaviour has often been interpreted as a way to subdue a female, reduce resistance, or lower the risk of cannibalism, but a broad review concludes that this explanation may be incomplete.</p>

<p>Different lineages show different anatomy and courtship patterns. Some males have modified telsons, venom-gland architecture, or sex-specific venom composition, suggesting that “sexual stinging” may not perform exactly the same function in every scorpion that uses it.</p>

<h2>Knock-out drug or love potion?</h2>

<p>The review contrasts a coercive hypothesis with a more speculative “love-potion” hypothesis. Venom components might stimulate mating behaviour, signal male quality, or provide another benefit rather than simply incapacitating the female.</p>

<p>Those possibilities are hypotheses, not established answers. The female's increasing docility during courtship could also involve pheromones or the normal progression of the ritual, and researchers still need direct behavioural and venom experiments.</p>

<h2>Why it matters</h2>

<p>Venom is often framed only as a weapon for prey capture or defence. Sexual stings show how evolution can recruit the same biochemical toolkit into reproduction, where conflict, cooperation, and communication may overlap.</p>
