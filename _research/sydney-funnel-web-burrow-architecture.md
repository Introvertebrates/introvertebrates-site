<h2>A famous spider with a largely hidden home</h2>

<p>
  The Sydney funnel-web, <em>Atrax robustus</em>, is usually introduced through its venom. Yet females and juveniles spend much of their lives inside burrows, making the architecture of those homes central to how they hunt, shelter and survive.
</p>

<p>
  Researchers studying wild populations in northern Sydney tagged <strong>170 occupied burrows</strong> across Lane Cove and Ku-ring-gai national parks and an adjacent site. They photographed the ground around entrances, measured the external silk and compared burrow locations with nearby control patches. At one site, they also created plaster casts of emptied burrows to reveal the structures beneath the soil.
</p>

<p>
  The result is an unusually physical look at a familiar spider: not simply where it occurs, but how rocks and roots become part of the home itself.
</p>

<h2>The spiders build beside something solid</h2>

<p>
  In the surface comparison, <strong>84% of sampled burrows were visibly built against a rock or tree</strong>. The immediate surroundings of burrows contained more exposed rock and tree structure than nearby control areas, supporting the idea that these were not random holes placed wherever soil happened to be available.
</p>

<p>
  The underground casts made the association even clearer. Every cast burrow followed a rock or tree root, including burrows whose anchor had been hidden below the surface. Some ran beneath more than 50 centimetres of sandstone; others curved around roots, stumps or gaps between boulders.
</p>

<p>
  The study describes this as a microhabitat association, not definitive proof that each spider consciously compares sites and selects the best one. Structural constraints could contribute to the pattern. Even so, the consistency above and below ground shows that solid features are fundamental to how these burrows are positioned and shaped.
</p>

<h2>A tunnel moulded by rock and root</h2>

<p>
  Rather than descending as neat vertical shafts, the burrows varied widely in direction, depth and form. Those beside sandstone generally followed the rock's horizontal plane, while burrows associated with trees tended to trace roots more vertically. Most consisted of a tunnel ending in a widened chamber, and several had a second chamber partway along.
</p>

<p>
  These anchors could offer several advantages. Sandstone can buffer temperature changes, and a shallow retreat beneath a rock may cost less to excavate than a deep unsupported shaft. Rocks and roots may also reinforce the tunnel or protect it from animals that scratch through leaf litter and topsoil.
</p>

<p>
  Those explanations remain hypotheses. The researchers did not experimentally test whether anchored burrows are cooler, safer or more successful. What the casts demonstrate directly is that the spider does not merely dig beside the structure—the structure helps determine the burrow's architecture.
</p>

<h2>Not every funnel-web builds an obvious funnel</h2>

<p>
  Every measured burrow had radiating silk triplines. These strong, non-sticky lines extend the spider's sensory reach beyond the entrance, transmitting vibrations when potential prey moves nearby. The longest recorded tripline reached 51 centimetres.
</p>

<p>
  The amount of additional silk varied substantially. Some entrances had little more than triplines; others included sheets of fine silk or extensive tubes. The iconic funnel shape appeared in only <strong>64% of the graded burrows</strong>.
</p>

<p>
  “Funnel-web” is therefore a useful family resemblance, not a promise that every occupied retreat will display a large, textbook funnel. The silk outside is a flexible structure, while the concealed tunnel and its solid anchor may be the more consistent parts of the home.
</p>

<h2>Dense neighbourhoods in the forest</h2>

<p>
  The researchers found aggregations containing between nine and 42 burrows. These clusters occurred in patches dominated by <em>Allocasuarina</em> trees, although the study was not designed to test landscape-wide habitat preferences and cannot establish that the trees themselves caused the aggregations.
</p>

<p>
  Such clustering matters because a sedentary spider's choice of home affects more than shelter. It can influence access to prey, exposure to heat and fire, and the proximity of future mates. Once a female has invested heavily in a silk-lined burrow, relocating is not equivalent to an orb-weaver rebuilding a web somewhere else the following night.
</p>

<h2>What the casts cannot tell us</h2>

<p>
  Plaster casting is destructive, so it was carried out cautiously on a limited set of burrows after the spiders had been removed. The surface comparisons were broader, but the work still represents three locations in northern Sydney rather than every habitat occupied by the species.
</p>

<p>
  The study also identifies associations rather than testing their fitness benefits. A rock-backed burrow may provide thermal stability, physical support or protection from predators, but those possibilities now need experiments and long-term monitoring.
</p>

<p>
  That restraint makes the central discovery no less striking. Beneath an entrance that may show only a few white threads, the spider's tunnel bends around the hidden landscape—using stone and root as both boundary and backbone.
</p>
