<h2>What was found</h2>

<p>Researchers monitored cardiac activity in two urban-tolerant orb weavers, the Jorō spider (<em>Trichonephila clavata</em>) and the golden silk spider (<em>Trichonephila clavipes</em>), while exposing them to traffic noise.</p>

<p>Jorō spiders with previous exposure to loud traffic showed the strongest heart-rate increase. Golden silk spiders responded differently, with changes more closely related to their baseline heart rate than to prior noise exposure.</p>

<h2>What makes that interesting</h2>

<p>Presence alone can make a species look unaffected by an urban environment. Physiology can reveal subtler effects, but the scale matters: the cardiac responses here were mild compared with those caused by severe stress.</p>

<p>The most accurate picture is therefore neither “traffic does nothing” nor “traffic is devastating them.” These spiders showed detectable responses while also appearing comparatively resilient.</p>

<h2>Why it matters</h2>

<p>Noise-pollution research has focused heavily on vertebrates. Measuring hidden responses in spiders broadens the picture and gives researchers a way to compare how different invertebrate species cope with human-altered environments.</p>
