Conservation · Wild populations

A collection should remember the wild.

Spiders are extraordinarily diverse, ecologically important, and still poorly known. Protecting them begins with habitat, good evidence, legal and traceable trade, and honest limits on what we know.

Read the label carefully

“Conservation status” can mean different things.

IUCN and CITES answer different questions. A species may appear in one system, both, or neither—and absence is not proof that it is secure.

IUCN

How close is a species to extinction?

The IUCN Red List evaluates extinction risk using categories such as Least Concern, Vulnerable, Endangered, and Critically Endangered. Many spiders have never been assessed.

CITES

Does international trade need control?

CITES regulates international trade in listed wildlife. An Appendix II listing does not by itself mean a species is endangered; it means trade must be controlled to avoid incompatible use.

Search the CITES checklist ↗

Names matter. Listings are tied to scientific names, and spider taxonomy changes. Check the currently accepted name, synonyms, geographic origin, and live legal listing before buying, selling, or moving an animal across a border.

Pressure in the wild

There is no single spider crisis.

Threats differ by species and place, but global expert assessments and field studies repeatedly point to habitat change, climate pressure, pollution, and unsustainable collection.

01

Habitat loss & fragmentation

Agriculture, logging, roads, urban growth, fire, and tourism can shrink or divide the exact microhabitats a spider depends on. Narrow-range species are especially exposed.

02

Climate change

Changing temperature, rainfall, drought, and extreme events can alter shelter, prey, and breeding conditions. Spiders with small ranges may have little room to move.

03

Pesticides & intensive land use

Broad land-management changes can reduce spider abundance and richness, while chemicals may affect spiders directly or through loss of prey and habitat complexity.

04

Collection & opaque trade

Wild collection can become an added pressure where demand is high, populations are small, or origin is concealed. Trade impacts cannot be separated from habitat and enforcement.

The honest version: spider conservation is limited by major data gaps. “Not assessed” and “data deficient” are descriptions of knowledge—not reassuring risk categories.

Tarantulas in trade

Captive-bred is a provenance claim—not a conservation badge.

Well-documented captive breeding can reduce demand for wild animals and give keepers a lawful alternative. But keeping a species in captivity does not automatically protect its habitat, preserve wild genetic diversity, or create a coordinated conservation population.

For several tarantulas, researchers have documented overlapping pressures from habitat loss, fragmentation, roads, tourism, and collection. Clear origin records are therefore part of responsible keeping—not paperwork to treat as optional.

Collection case · Brachypelma hamorii

What Appendix II actually says

Brachypelma species are included in CITES Appendix II. International commercial trade may be allowed, but it is regulated and should be supported by the required permits and lawful acquisition records.

It does mean
International trade is controlled.
It does not mean
Every listed species is currently endangered.

A keeper’s part

Make every animal traceable.

The most useful habits are quiet ones: asking better questions, keeping records, following law, and refusing to turn uncertainty into a sales claim.

  1. 01

    Prefer documented captive-bred animals.

    Ask who bred the animal, when and where it was bred, and whether the seller can explain its chain of custody.

  2. 02

    Keep the exact scientific name.

    Record the name used at purchase, later taxonomic changes, breeder or seller, date, locality data if legitimate, and any permit or receipt numbers.

  3. 03

    Check rules before movement or sale.

    CITES governs international trade; national and local wildlife, animal-health, and invasive-species rules may add further requirements.

  4. 04

    Never release a captive animal.

    Release can move pathogens, disrupt local populations, or introduce a non-native species. Rehome responsibly if keeping is no longer possible.

  5. 05

    Do not invent locality or conservation value.

    Unknown origin should stay unknown. A captive lineage is not a conservation programme unless it is managed toward defined conservation goals.

  6. 06

    Support habitat and expertise.

    Back field research, habitat protection, local conservation organisations, taxonomic work, and evidence-based education—not only charismatic species.
