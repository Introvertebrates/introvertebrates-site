Conservation

A life in care. A place in the wild.

The spiders we know by name belong to species with wild habitats, ecological roles and pressures of their own. Being familiar in collections does not tell us how a species is doing in nature.

What the assessments say.

Each entry gives the assessment’s scope and year. Sources checked 9 September 2026; older assessments are not new population surveys.

Ruby

Chromatopelma cyaneopubescens

Endangered · Venezuela · 2015

Venezuela’s 2015 Red Book lists Ruby’s species as Endangered (EN). It identifies habitat loss and removal from the wild for trade as threats. This is a Venezuelan national assessment, not a global IUCN category.

Provita · Venezuelan Red Book (2015) ↗

A small place on the map.

The 2015 Red Book describes Ruby’s species as endemic to Venezuela’s Paraguaná Peninsula, living among shrubs and herbaceous vegetation and lining crevices or holes with silk. Urban expansion and removal for trade threaten that wild setting.

What remains unknown?

That assessment highlights a lack of population studies and calls for surveys of abundance, distribution and microhabitat. Its date matters: these are the gaps described in 2015, not a claim that no research has happened since. Success in a terrarium cannot tell us whether wild populations are recovering.

Sabrina

Brachypelma hamorii

Vulnerable · IUCN global · 2018

The official CEC tarantula guide records a 2018 IUCN assessment of Vulnerable (VU) for Sabrina’s species. Vulnerable is one of the threatened categories; it is distinct from Endangered. The guide also lists the species in CITES Appendix II.

CEC · CITES-listed tarantula guide ↗

Beyond the familiar red knees.

The CEC guide places Sabrina’s species in western Mexico, from southeastern Jalisco through coastal Colima to northwestern coastal Michoacán. Its account documents historical collection for export and a long history of animals traded under the name B. smithi.

Why the name matters.

Those mixed identities complicate interpretation of older trade records. A familiar appearance or a shop label is not a population survey. Keeping accurate species names and origin records helps avoid carrying that confusion forward.

Read the labels carefully

Risk, knowledge and protection.

Threatened has a specific meaning.

IUCN groups Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered as threatened. A national assessment describes risk within that country; a global assessment considers the species worldwide.

IUCN · Red List categories ↗

Trade listings answer another question.

A CITES Appendix II listing concerns regulation of international trade. It does not, on its own, mean a species is classified as Endangered. The extinction-risk category and the trade listing should be read separately.

CEC · official identification guide ↗

A missing label is not an all-clear.

Not Evaluated means a species has not been assessed against the criteria. Data Deficient means the available evidence is inadequate to judge extinction risk. Neither means “safe.” An uncertain identification is a separate problem: an informal hobby name cannot automatically inherit a named species’ assessment. If we have not verified a status, we say so rather than assigning a category.

Practical keeper choices

Ask about the animal’s story.

These are practical recommendations from Introvertebrates, informed by the conservation and trade sources above.

Before acquiring an animal

Ask who bred it, whether its origin is documented, and how its identity was established. Prefer documented captive breeding where available. “Captive-bred” is a claim worth checking, not a substitute for a traceable history.

Keep the history with the animal

Retain acquisition records, the seller’s stated origin, identification changes and breeding records. Pass relevant information to the next keeper. Record uncertainty honestly rather than turning an assumption into a fact.

Support work in the wild

Look for projects that explain their field methods, local partners and results. Habitat work and population surveys address questions a collection cannot answer. The IUCN Red List explains how to support species assessments; this is a resource, not an Introvertebrates partnership.

Breeding with a purpose

What does success actually protect?

Captive breeding can produce animals without taking those individuals from the wild and can build useful husbandry knowledge. Whether it reduces pressure on wild populations is a separate question that needs evidence. It does not preserve a species’ habitat or demonstrate recovery in nature.

Before calling a breeding effort conservation, ask: what wild-population problem does it address, who is responsible for the work, and how will its benefit be measured? Introvertebrates presents its collection as animal keeping, observation and education. A successful breeding is worth documenting without claiming a conservation outcome that has not been demonstrated.

From curiosity to care

Keep the wild part of the story.

Habitat protection and field research matter alongside good care in captivity. Our aim is to make the wild species visible behind the individual animal: where it lives, what threatens it, and what we still need to learn. Keeping a species is not, by itself, a conservation programme.
