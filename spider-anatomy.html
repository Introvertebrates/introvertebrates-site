Spider anatomy

Two body regions. An extraordinary machine.

Spiders are arachnids, not insects. Their compact design joins a sensory and mechanical front section to an abdomen that houses much of the animal’s internal machinery—including silk production.

Body map

Begin with the parts you can see.

The markers identify broad dorsal landmarks on a male Ceratogyrus darlingi. Fine structures and ventral organs are explained below rather than placed where the photograph cannot show them.

1

Prosoma

Also called the cephalothorax. It carries the eyes, chelicerae, pedipalps, and four pairs of walking legs beneath a firm dorsal carapace.

2

Abdomen

The opisthosoma is softer and usually unsegmented externally. It contains much of the digestive, reproductive, circulatory, respiratory, and silk-producing anatomy.

3

Eyes & mouthparts

Simple eyes sit near the front. Below them, chelicerae carry the fangs; pedipalps help with sensing, prey handling, and reproduction in mature males.

4

Eight walking legs

Each leg has seven main segments from coxa to tarsus. Muscles and internal fluid pressure work together to move different joints.

Under the surface

The rest of the system.

Some of a spider’s most important structures are too small, too ventral, or too internal to identify responsibly in a dorsal photograph.

Chelicerae and fangs

The paired chelicerae are the first appendages. Each ends in a fang connected to a venom gland. They grasp and puncture prey; spiders take in liquefied food rather than chewing with jaws.

Pedipalps

The shorter pair beside the chelicerae are richly sensory. In adult males, the tips are modified into reproductive organs, often making sex recognition possible from an external view.

Spinnerets and silk

Movable spinnerets sit at the rear of the abdomen. Different silk glands can produce materials for retreats, draglines, egg sacs, prey capture, and vibration-sensitive structures.

Respiration

Book lungs open on the underside of the abdomen. Some spider groups also have a tracheal system. These structures exchange gases while remaining protected beneath the body.

Sensory hairs

Ordinary-looking setae can detect touch and air movement. Slit-like strain sensors in the exoskeleton respond to mechanical forces, giving spiders an exquisitely detailed sense of vibration.

Growth and molting

A rigid exoskeleton cannot expand continuously. A spider periodically forms a new one beneath the old, sheds the old cuticle, and expands before the replacement hardens.
