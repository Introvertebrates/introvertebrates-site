Spider anatomy

Two body regions. An extraordinary machine.

Spiders are arachnids, not insects. Their compact design joins a sensory and mechanical front section to an abdomen that houses much of the animal’s internal machinery—including silk production.

Body map

Begin with the parts you can see.

The markers identify broad dorsal landmarks on a male Ceratogyrus darlingi. Fine structures and ventral organs are explained below rather than placed where this photograph cannot show them.

1

Prosoma

Also called the cephalothorax. It carries the eyes, chelicerae, pedipalps, and four pairs of walking legs beneath a firm dorsal carapace.

2

Abdomen

The opisthosoma is softer and usually unsegmented externally. It contains much of the digestive, reproductive, circulatory, respiratory, and silk-producing anatomy.

3

Eyes & mouthparts

Simple eyes sit near the front. Below them, chelicerae carry the fangs; pedipalps help with sensing, prey handling, and reproduction in mature males.

4

Eight walking legs

Each leg has seven main segments from coxa to tarsus. Muscles and internal fluid pressure work together to move different joints.

Verified close looks

Show only what the evidence can support.

Real photographs are used where the archive shows a structure clearly. Topics stay text-only when the available photographs do not support a reliable visual explanation.

Eye cluster

Most spiders have eight simple eyes, though the number and arrangement vary. Larger and smaller eyes catch the light together in this macro, but the angle still does not make all eight equally easy to count.

Chelicerae and fangs

The paired chelicerae are the first appendages. Each ends in a fang connected to a venom gland; both dark, curved fangs are visible between the reddish cheliceral bases here. They grasp and puncture prey, while the spider takes in liquefied food rather than chewing with jaws.

Pedipalps

The shorter appendage pair beside the chelicerae is richly sensory and helps handle prey. In adult males, the tips become specialised reproductive organs called palpal bulbs. This front view makes the position of the pedipalps especially easy to compare with the surrounding walking legs.

Silk and webbing

Movable spinnerets sit at the rear of the abdomen. Different silk glands make materials for retreats, draglines, egg sacs, prey capture, and vibration-sensitive structures. This photograph makes the layered webbing especially clear, though the spinnerets themselves remain out of view.

Ventral anatomy and respiration

Mygalomorph spiders such as tarantulas usually have two pairs of book lungs. The four pale regions on this abdomen mark those paired respiratory structures; the narrow openings lead inward, while the gas-exchanging lamellae remain hidden inside.

Sensory hairs

Some specialised setae detect touch or air movement, while slit-like strain sensors in the exoskeleton respond to mechanical forces. Together they give spiders a detailed sense of nearby movement and vibration. The macro shows the hairs clearly, but appearance alone cannot reveal the function of each one.

Growth and molting

A rigid exoskeleton cannot expand continuously. A spider forms a new one beneath the old, lies on its back or side, withdraws from the old cuticle, and expands before the replacement hardens.
